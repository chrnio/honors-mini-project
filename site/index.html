Problem Statement 16

News broadcasts are full of words whose meaning shifts with context. bank can be a riverbank or a financial institution. interest can mean curiosity or a loan charge. plant can be a factory or something you grow in soil. A transcript‑processing system that cannot tell these apart gets the underlying facts wrong, even when it transcribed every word correctly.

This project asks a narrow, answerable version of that question: for eight frequently ambiguous words (bankinterestplantrunstatecaseratepower), can a classifier learn which dictionary sense is meant in a sentence, using nothing but the word's part of speech and the words sitting near it?

Step by Step Explanation

1
Start from sentences with known answers
SemCor is a corpus where every word is already tagged with its correct dictionary sense (from WordNet). Every sentence containing one of the eight target words becomes one labelled example.
2
Look only at the neighbours
For each occurrence, take a window of nearby words — either 2 or 5 words on each side — and record their part‑of‑speech tags plus a simple word count (bag‑of‑words). The target word itself is never shown its own meaning, only its surroundings.
3
Turn that into numbers
Those tags and word counts become a feature vector — a row of numbers a classifier can actually learn from, built fresh for every single occurrence of every target word.
4
Train two different classifiers per word
A linear Support Vector Machine and a Naive Bayes model are each trained separately for every target word, since "bank" and "plant" have entirely different sets of possible senses.
5
Test fairly, then compare
5‑fold cross‑validation splits the examples into 5 groups, training on 4 and testing on the 1 left out, rotating until every example has been tested once — so no model is ever graded on sentences it already memorised. Accuracy and macro F1 are then compared across both classifiers and both window sizes.

Dataset Details and Parameters

Training data comes from SemCor, a subset of the Brown Corpus in which every content word is hand‑annotated with its correct WordNet synset. Every sentence containing one of the eight target words contributes one labelled instance. Senses occurring fewer than 5 times for a word are dropped before training, since 5‑fold cross‑validation needs at least one example of a class in every fold to learn or test it at all — keeping them in would only add noise to the macro F1 score.

Parameters and Why They Were Chosen

1
Context window: ±2 and ±5 tokens
Both sizes are tested rather than picking one. A narrow window captures immediate grammar (the word right before or after); a wide window captures the broader topic of the sentence. Comparing the two shows which effect actually drives each word's meaning.
2
Minimum sense frequency: 5 instances
Any sense with fewer than 5 labelled examples is dropped before training. Below that count, 5‑fold cross‑validation can't reliably put at least one example of the sense in every fold, so keeping it would just inject noise into the results.
3
5‑fold stratified cross‑validation, fixed seed
Stratified folds keep each sense's share of examples roughly the same across all 5 folds, which matters because the senses are far from evenly distributed. A fixed random seed (42) makes the split, and therefore the comparison between models, exactly reproducible.
4
Features: neighbouring POS tags + bag‑of‑words counts
A dictionary vectorizer turns each occurrence's part‑of‑speech tags and nearby word counts into a numeric vector. Counts (rather than yes/no flags) are used so a word that appears twice nearby counts for more than one that appears once.
5
Normalization: lowercasing, lemmatizing, stopword removal
"Run", "running" and "ran" are collapsed into one feature so the model isn't forced to learn each inflected form separately, and common function words (the, of, and, ...) are dropped since they carry no topical signal about which sense is meant.
6
Metric: macro F1 alongside accuracy
Plain accuracy can look good just by always guessing the most common sense. Macro F1 scores every sense equally regardless of how often it appears, which is what actually shows whether a model learned the rarer meanings too.
7
Models: linear SVM and Multinomial Naive Bayes
The linear SVM is a strong, standard choice for sparse high‑dimensional text features like these. Naive Bayes is kept as a simple, fast baseline, to see how much the SVM's extra complexity actually buys over an assumption as crude as feature independence.
